# 2 problem · angle 1
**Charitable money is warehoused, and donors never find out where it went.**
0% — legally required annual payout for a commercial DAF. Fidelity Charitable took in ~$16B in 2024.
35% — share of DAF accounts that paid out nothing in a studied year.
5% — the minimum a private foundation must release. Lilly ($79.9B) and Gates ($78.7B) hold ~44% of the top-50's assets.

Blurb: the deduction is taken the day the money leaves; the gift can wait forever. Meanwhile it sits
invested — the donor's share and the public's, because a deduction is tax that was never paid. Most
people give modestly, at tax time, and never learn that most of what they gave is sitting in corporate equity.
Second line: the totals flatter it — a deposit counts as giving the day it is deducted, not the day it
arrives — and a foundation can meet its 5% by granting into a DAF, which owes nothing.

# 3 problem · angle 2
**The money that does move is barely policed.**
50¢ — in half of 588 audited New York campaigns (2024) the charity kept under fifty cents on the dollar; 104 lost money.
$16.25 — value booked for a deworming pill that costs about 2¢. Overvalued goods turn a 65% program ratio into 95%.
734% — one charity's program spending overstated, $7.75M against $929k, using an accounting rule that permits it.
10 pts — how far reported overhead is understated sector-wide (peer-reviewed, 2024).

Blurb: none of this is hidden. It is disclosed, audited and legal — the rules permit the valuation, the
allocation and the solicitation. What is missing is any consequence for using them.

# 4 insight
**People are generous — and hesitant to give without options, control, and a reason to look.**
Four cards, each naming the missing or misused media:
- *No options, and nobody to tell you* — finding something worth funding is unpaid research. No beat, no critic, no column, so the winner is whoever asked loudest. Advertising is the only media most causes get.
- *No control, and no follow-up* — you cannot vote on the target and nothing reports back. The only account of your donation is written by the people who took it.
- *Nobody is watching — and not by accident* — a $600B sector with no beat. Maybe the silence isn't because charity is boring; maybe the people it would embarrass prefer you think so.
- *Attention is the standard now* — everything that wants participation has a network and content behind it. Charity still asks the way institutions asked in 1985.

Blurb: an election gives options and control. A newsroom gives feedback and a reason to look. So we
built both into the donation itself — and what makes it worth watching is what keeps it honest.
*edits*

# 5 process
**Every mission is elected twice.**
1 · Mission Election — benefactors commit to an initiative inside the week's cause. The winner becomes the mission; nothing is chosen yet but the idea.
2 · Organization Election — benefactors elect the organization that will run it, on its record and its statement, before any plan exists.
3 · The mission runs — money releases in stages against a published budget, and Earthbux News reports each one.

Band 1: **the Forum runs underneath all of it, not between the votes.** It elects the causes before any
of this begins, argues the initiatives while the race is open, and carries on afterwards as research,
itemized budgets and progress review.
Band 2: then the three of us are bound to one mission — Earthbux reports, the organization executes,
the benefactors deliberate — and **every step is approved before it happens**. We capture the donor and
the media consumer in the same motion.
*edits*

# 6 money model
**Where every dollar sits — and who still controls it.**
Split bar: ~30% awarded to the elected organization · ≤30% ceiling on what Earthbux can ever consume ·
~40% stays as benefactor-held EBX, released as the mission proves itself.
- *One coin per mission* — donate and your money becomes mission-specific EBX. Many sources converge on one mission and you track all of it by looking at that mission's coin. Only about a tenth crosses at the election; most stays under benefactor control well into the mission.
- *Power to the small donor* — everyone is granted $1 a week, spendable only in that week's elections. Each further block of ten counts for half the block before it (10 → 10.00, 20 → 15.00, 40 → 18.75).
- *Being early is what pays* — right about the initiative → lowest cost basis; voted at all → middling; donating later → higher.
*edits*

# 7 justification
**The newsroom is the moat.**
Flywheel drawn: donations → coverage → trust → more benefactors → bigger pool → better missions → back round.
Centre line: the audit is what makes the next vote worth casting.
- *Every donation passes through layers* — two votes · a signed claim attestation · identity verification by the newsroom · a community-approved budget · release step by step against published reports, ours beside theirs.
- *Leaving is feedback, not leakage* — benefactors keep control of most of their money well into the mission; holding it, moving it or taking it back is a public signal no grantmaker has ever had. None of what they build here ports anywhere else.

# 13 cost analysis
**The grant is the cost. Scrutiny is the revenue.**
$1.00/week granted per benefactor is a real cost; what comes back is our share of the pool that dollar
joins, priced by oversight (1/16 dormant → 5/16 arbitrated).
| purchased/wk | pooled | our share | net |
| $0 | $1.00 | $0.31 | −$0.69 |
| $2 | $3.00 | $0.94 | −$0.06 |
| $5 | $6.00 | $1.88 | +$0.88 |
Break-even purchased commitment: $2.20/wk at 5/16, $4.33 at 3/16, $15.00 at 1/16.
Newsroom covered at roughly 10,000 weekly-active benefactors in the $5 scenario; the $2 scenario never
gets there.
Two levers: a granted token costs nothing until committed, and our share rises with the scrutiny a
mission needs.
