The problem · angle 1

Charitable money is warehoused,
and donors never find out where it went.

0%
Legally required annual payout for a commercial donor-advised fund. Fidelity Charitable took in ~$16B in 2024.
35%
Share of DAF accounts that paid out nothing in a studied year.
5%
The minimum a private foundation must release. Lilly ($79.9B) and Gates ($78.7B) hold ~44% of the top-50's assets.

The deduction is taken the day the money leaves; the gift can wait forever. Meanwhile it sits invested — the donor's share and the public's, because a deduction is tax that was never paid. Most people give modestly, at tax time, and never learn that most of what they gave is sitting in corporate equity.

And the totals flatter it: a deposit counts as giving the day it is deducted, not the day it arrives — while a foundation can meet its 5% by granting into a DAF, which owes nothing. The money moves, and still hasn't gone anywhere.

The problem · angle 2

The money that does move
is barely policed.

50¢

In half of 588 audited New York campaigns (2024), the charity kept under fifty cents on the dollar. 104 lost money outright.

$16.25

Value booked for a deworming pill that costs about 2¢. Overvalued goods turn a 65% program ratio into 95%.

734%

One charity's program spending overstated — $7.75M reported against $929k — using an accounting rule that permits it.

10 pts

How far reported overhead is understated sector-wide (peer-reviewed, 2024) — the one number donors are told to check.

None of this is hidden. It is disclosed, audited and legal — the rules permit the valuation, the allocation and the solicitation. What is missing is any consequence for using them.

The insight

People are generous — and hesitant to give
without options, control, and a reason to look.

No options, and nobody to tell you

Finding something worth funding is unpaid research. No beat, no critic, no column — so the winner is whoever asked loudest. Advertising is the only media most causes get.

No control, and no follow-up

You cannot vote on the target, and nothing reports back. The only account of your donation is written by the people who took it.

Nobody is watching — and not by accident

A $600B sector with no beat. Maybe the silence isn't because charity is boring — maybe the people it would embarrass prefer you think so.

Attention is the standard now

Everything that wants participation has a network and content behind it. Charity still asks the way institutions asked in 1985.

An election gives options and control. A newsroom gives feedback and a reason to look. So we built both into the donation itself — and what makes it worth watching is what keeps it honest.

How it works

Every mission is elected twice.

1 · Mission Election
Benefactors commit to an initiative inside the week's cause. The winner becomes the mission. Nothing is chosen yet but the idea.
›
2 · Organization Election
Benefactors elect the organization that will run it — on its record and its statement, before any plan exists.
›
3 · The mission runs
Money releases in stages against a published budget, and Earthbux News reports each one.

The Forum runs underneath all of it, not between the votes. It elects the causes before any of this begins, argues the initiatives while the race is open, and carries on afterwards as research, itemized budgets and progress review.

Then the three of us are bound to one mission: Earthbux reports, the organization executes, the benefactors deliberate — and every step is approved before it happens. We capture the donor and the media consumer in the same motion.

The money model

Where every dollar sits — and who still controls it.

~30%  awarded to the elected organization
≤30%  ceiling on what Earthbux can ever consume
~40%  stays as benefactor-held EBX, released as the mission proves itself

One coin per mission

Donate and your money becomes mission-specific EBX. Many sources converge on one mission, and you track all of it by looking at that mission's coin — its value moves with what the mission actually resolves.

Only about a tenth crosses at the election. Most of the money stays under the benefactors' control well into the mission, and is released against published budgets and reports.

Power to the small donor

Everyone is granted $1 a week to allocate — free, and spendable only in that week's elections. Buying more never buys proportional power: each additional block of ten counts for half the block before it (10 → 10.00, 20 → 15.00, 40 → 18.75).

Being early is what pays

Right about the initiative → the lowest cost basis · voted at all → a middling basis · donating later → a higher one. Conviction is priced before consensus is.

EARTHBUX

Why it holds

The newsroom is the moat.

Anyone can build a voting UI. Almost nobody can build the standing to audit where the money went, and that audit is why the next vote matters.

Every donation passes through layers

Two votes · a signed claim attestation · identity verification by the newsroom · a community-approved budget · release step by step against published reports, ours beside theirs.

Leaving is feedback, not leakage

Benefactors keep control of most of their money well into the mission — and holding it, moving it or taking it back is a public signal no grantmaker has ever had. None of what they build here — history, coins, memberships — ports anywhere else.

Cost analysis

The grant is the cost.
Scrutiny is the revenue.

Every benefactor is granted $1.00 a week — a real cost, not revenue. What comes back is our share of the pool that dollar joins, priced by oversight: 1/16 for a dormant mission up to 5/16 for one being arbitrated.

$0 — grant only$1.00$0.31−$0.69

At the full 5/16 oversight share. Break-even purchased commitment: $2.20 a week at 5/16, $4.33 at 3/16, $15.00 at 1/16 — the quieter the mission, the more it has to be bought.

When does the pool cover the newsroom?

Six roles at [$450k] a year — about $8,650 a week — needs roughly 10,000 weekly-active benefactors at the $5 scenario. The $2 scenario never gets there: this does not work on free grants alone.

Two levers, before headcount

A granted token costs nothing until it is committed, so the grant bill only arrives when the product is working. And our share rises with the scrutiny a mission needs — the missions that cost most to watch pay for the watching.

Challenge these: salary placeholder, no infrastructure line, unset tranche split.

Milestones

Four quarters to a closed loop.

Q1 · Standing
Entity, charitable-status filing, banking. The mission page ships and the simulation runs against it. Gate: the legal track is open, not just the code.
Q2 · Counterparties
Organization registration, verification and outbound contact go live. First cohort signed; first mission claimed under the attestation. Gate: a real organization on the record.
Q3 · Money moves
First mission funded end to end: budget published, steps agreed, released in tranches. Gate: the first check.
Q4 · The loop closes
First independent audit published by a paid journalist. Retention measured across cycles; [N] weekly-active benefactors. Gate: a story about what the money did.

The legal track runs beside all four, not inside them. 501(c)(3) or fiscal sponsorship, the deductibility of each tranche, and the claim agreement itself. It has the longest lead time of anything on this slide and none of it is engineering.
