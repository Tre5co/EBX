## Part 0 — Four fixes to your Section 1 draft

**1. The foundation-to-DAF figure is wrong.** You wrote $2.1B. It is **$2.6 billion in 2021** ([Inequality.org/IPS](https://inequality.org/article/private-foundations-dafs-2021/)) and **$3.2 billion in 2022** ([IPS Independent Report on DAFs, 2025](https://ips-dc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Independent-Report-on-DAFs-FINAL-04-03-2025.pdf)). Use the 2022 number — it is newer and larger. The $2.5B DAF-to-DAF figure for 2021 is correct, and the same IPS report puts DAF-to-DAF at **$4.4B in 2023** and **over $10.2B cumulative 2020–2023** between national sponsors alone.

**2. "100,000+ foundations" → ~120,000**, holding **~$1.8 trillion** (2025). Say the exact number; the round-up reads as a guess.

**3. The 20% rule needs one clause.** It is 20% for the **foundation and all disqualified persons combined** — 35% where an unrelated third party has effective control, with a 2% de minimis for the foundation alone. And the **Newman's Own exception (2018)** lets a foundation own **100%** of a wholly-owned business that distributes all profits — which is why Newman's Own is in your example list, and worth one sentence, because it is the deliberate carve-out.

**4. "often making annual returns of 10% or more"** needs a citation or a hedge. What you can say without one: the payout is calculated on *assets*, not income, so any year the endowment returns more than ~5% net of the payout, the corpus grows. Perpetuity is the arithmetic default. That is the point, and it needs no return assumption.

## Part 1 — Section 2 draft: Pooled / pass-through vehicles

### Draft prose

> **2. Pooled and pass-through vehicles**
>
> Examples: Fidelity Charitable, National Philanthropic Trust, DAFgiving360 (formerly Schwab), Vanguard Charitable, Silicon Valley Community Foundation, DonorsTrust
>
> A donor-advised fund is a charitable account held by a sponsoring organization. The donor gets the deduction the moment money goes in and keeps advisory control over where it goes afterward — with **no deadline and no payout requirement whatsoever**. DAFs now hold **$327.9 billion** across **3.59 million accounts**, taking in **$90.6 billion** and paying out **$64.6 billion** in FY2024. Roughly **23% of all individual giving** now enters a DAF rather than a working charity, and the Institute for Policy Studies projects that could reach 50% by 2028.
>
> The tax treatment is better than a private foundation's in every respect: cash is deductible to 60% of AGI (vs. 30%), appreciated securities to 30% (vs. 20%), and appreciated stock is deducted at **full fair market value** where a private foundation would often be limited to cost basis. The DAF gets the superior deduction and none of the payout obligation.
>
> Sponsors advertise an aggregate payout of **25.3%**, which sounds like the opposite of warehousing. That number is an artifact of how it is calculated. Account-level research on **57,539 accounts across 111 programs** found **37% made no grant at all in an average year**, and a **median three-year payout of 9%** — against the ~24% aggregate. Roughly 22% of accounts granted nothing across three consecutive years. The aggregate is inflated by a small number of high-velocity accounts, by new inflows that pass straight through, and by counting **DAF-to-DAF transfers as grants** — $4.4 billion of them in 2023. Recomputed on the IRS-preferred formula, median sponsor payout is **9.7%**, and **community foundations are the lowest of any sponsor type at 9.0%** — the opposite of their reputation.
>
> Disclosure is the other half. A sponsor files **one Form 990 for the entire institution**; individual accounts have no filing, no EIN, and no public identity. Schedule D asks only for aggregate counts. Grants can be made anonymously — Fidelity Charitable reports **5% of its 2025 grants were fully anonymous** and another 15% carried an account name only, which on $18.3 billion of grants is roughly $900 million of untraceable money from one sponsor in one year. Analysis of 2020–2022 filings found DAFs support politically engaged charities at a **70% higher rate** than other funders and account for **more than a quarter of all hate-group funding**.
>
> This is now where the money is. **Eleven of the top twenty American charities by revenue are DAF sponsors**, taking **$67 billion of the $91 billion** the top twenty raised in 2024 — 74%. Fidelity Charitable has been the single largest recipient of charitable contributions in the United States for **nine consecutive years**, having displaced United Way in 2016. The largest actual operating charity, Feeding America, raises about one-third of what Fidelity does.
>
> Legislation to fix this — the Accelerating Charitable Efforts (ACE) Act, which would have imposed 15- and 50-year distribution deadlines and barred foundation-to-DAF transfers from counting as payout — was introduced in 2021, stalled, and **has not been reintroduced**. Proposed Treasury regulations from November 2023 remain proposed, and would in any case impose no payout requirement and no per-account disclosure. Meanwhile a **fiscal sponsorship** transparency bill requiring project-level disclosure passed House Ways and Means in July 2026. Congress is moving to regulate the smallest pass-through vehicle in the sector and not the one moving twenty times the money.

### The counting question you asked about — the answer is *partly*

You were right that DAFs land in the individual category, but the double-count works differently than it does for foundations, and getting this wrong is the kind of error that gets a piece dismissed.

**Giving USA's actual treatment**, in their own methodology language:

> "Donor-advised funds are only counted as sources of giving if they are housed in community foundations — otherwise, we do not count grantmaking *from* donor-advised funds in our sources calculations."

So:

| Route | Counted on the way in | Counted again on the way out |
|---|---|---|
| Fidelity, NPT, DAFgiving360, Vanguard | Individual giving | **No** |
| **Community-foundation DAF** | Individual giving | **Yes — as "Giving by Foundations"** |
| Private foundation | Individual giving | **Yes — as "Giving by Foundations"** |

**The clean double-count is the foundation route and the community-foundation DAF route.** Commercial DAFs are counted once. Say it that way and nobody can knock it down.

On the *recipient* side Giving USA nets DAFs — and the netting numbers are themselves the story. For 2024: gross direct support to Public-Society Benefit was **$117.1 billion**, of which **$78.8 billion was gifts into DAF accounts** and **$52.7 billion was grants back out**, netting to **$26.0 billion**. Two-thirds of gross giving to an entire recipient sector is DAF plumbing. ([CCS Fundraising summary of the Giving USA 2026 webinar](https://www.ccsfundraising.com/insights/perspectives-on-philanthropy-giving-usa-2026/))

⚠️ Flag: CCS reports the netted 2024 PSB figure as $64.6B; Giving USA's published figure is **$66.84B**. Use the published number for the category and CCS for the decomposition, and say which is which.

Directly relevant to Earthbux — this is the exact failure mode a pooled platform can fall into.

## Part 2 — The religion question, answered

You asked whether religion really receives so much more than everything else. **The number is real and consistently produced. It is also narrower than readers assume, unauditable in a way no other category is, and mostly not spent on what readers picture.** Three separate findings; keep them separate.

### Finding 1: the number is real, and smaller than it used to be

2025: **religion $151.58B** of **$617.20B total** — the largest subsector for forty consecutive years, ahead of human services ($99.50B) and education ($92.01B). But real growth was **−0.2%** in 2025 and **−1.0%** in 2024. The share has fallen from **53% in 1987** to **32% in 2014** to **23–25% now**.

⚠️ Two shares circulate and they are not the same. Giving USA's published pie says **23%**; religion ÷ total giving is **24.6%**. The recipient categories sum to $666.3B against a $617.2B total, reconciled by a **negative ~$49.1B "unallocated giving"** line. "About a quarter" is safe. If you print 23%, say it is 23% of allocated recipient dollars.

⚠️ Do not print "62% in 1984." It circulates and no primary source supports it. The best-sourced early figure is 53% (1987), from Giving USA's own then-chairman.

### Finding 2: the category is *narrower* than readers think — and this cuts against the skeptical reading

Giving USA's religion category covers **congregations, denominations, missionary societies, and religious media**. That is all. **Catholic Charities, World Vision, the Salvation Army, Lutheran Services, Islamic Relief, Yeshiva University and religious hospitals are counted elsewhere** — under human services, health, education, or international affairs.

So the honest framing is the reverse of the usual suspicion: the 23–25% figure is **not padded with hospitals**. It is a narrow measure of giving to worship institutions. Under a definition that *does* include faith-based nonprofits, religious giving is **73% of all US charitable giving** ([IU Lilly Family School](https://blog.philanthropy.indianapolis.iu.edu/2020/01/21/giving-by-people-of-faith-and-why-it-matters-for-fundraising/)).

### Finding 3: this is the one — the number is not auditable, and most of it stays inside the building

**Congregations are exempt from filing Form 990** under 26 U.S.C. § 6033(a)(3)(A)(i), and are not required to apply for 501(c)(3) recognition at all. There is no return-level data for the great majority of the money. Giving USA's own description of its universe is ~1.1 million registered charities "plus a conservative estimate of 300,000 American religious organizations."

**How the estimate is actually built**, per the Center on Philanthropy's own 2002 methods paper: the baseline is a **1986 Independent Sector survey of congregational revenues**, rolled forward using denominational self-reports in the National Council of Churches' *Yearbook of American and Canadian Churches* — which **ceased publication in 2012**. A peer-reviewed 2019 paper (Mundey, King & Fulton, *Social Compass*) states that Giving USA still "relies on the outdated 1986 national survey." The methods paper is explicit that there are **no formal error bounds** — unlike other subsectors, the religion estimate carries no standard error. A 1991–1999 recalculation with fuller data revised religion **down by $10.54 billion cumulatively**.

⚠️ **I could not find any public documentation of what replaced the Yearbook after 2012, or any current published estimating equation.** That gap is itself your story. The single highest-value reporting step available is to ask Giving USA and the Lilly Family School, on the record, to state the current religion equation and its inputs.

**Where the money goes once it arrives.** Four independent datasets converge:

| Source | Personnel | Buildings | Programs | Outward mission/benevolence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NSCEP (n=1,231, 2017 data) | 49% | 23% | 10% | **11%** (+6% denominational dues) |
| FACT (n=15,278, 2020) | 44% | 26% | 11% | **13%** |
| empty tomb (2015) | — | — | — | **16%** (was 21% in 1968) |

And the sharpest number, from the National Congregations Study: **the median congregation spends about $1,500 a year — roughly 2% of budget — directly on social service activities**, even though 83% of congregations do some social service work.

⚠️ **Print the counterweight or you will be corrected.** The same authors (Chaves & Eagle, *Religions*, 2016) write that "between 15 and 30 percent of congregational income is spent in ways that benefit non-members" — the gap between 2% and 15–30% is staff time on community work, free building use by outside groups, and transfers to denominational service arms. Real benefit a budget-line analysis misses.

**Defensible sentence:** *formal line-item outward charitable spending by US congregations runs roughly 11–16% of budget, about 2% for dedicated social-service programs, and a broader contested measure of spending benefiting non-members runs 15–30%. Roughly three-quarters goes to staff and buildings.*

### The transparency gap is actively widening — this is the freshest angle

Large ministries that *did* file 990s are reclassifying as "associations of churches" and going dark. **More than 80 of the 1,000 largest US Christian ministries have sought that status since 2020**; over 100 now hold it — including **Focus on the Family, Compassion International, Convoy of Hope, and the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association**. Wycliffe Associates last filed in 2008; Ravi Zacharias International Ministries stopped in 2015, with misconduct allegations surfacing by 2017 and the organization shutting down by 2022. A Trinity Foundation investigation identified **60+ religious nonprofits holding $1.8 billion that stopped filing** ([Christianity Today, April 2025](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/04/when-church-is-not-church-ministries-nonprofits-irs-tax-status/); [The Roys Report, July 2025](https://roysreport.com/dark-money-billions-dollars-ministry-assets-no-longer-reported-irs/)).

**Enforcement barely exists.** § 7611 requires an "appropriate high-level Treasury official" to authorize any church inquiry, and *U.S. v. Living Word Christian Center* (D. Minn. 2009) held the IRS Director of Exempt Organizations is not high-level enough — which effectively shut down enforcement. The 2007–2011 Grassley televangelist investigation of six ministries ended with **no penalties and no definitive findings; four of the six never provided complete financial information**.

**The one clean documented case: Ensign Peak Advisors / LDS Church, SEC, February 21, 2023.** $5,000,000 total penalty ($4M + $1M), **13 shell LLCs**, ~**$32 billion** concealed, conduct spanning 1997–2019, charged under Exchange Act §13(f) and Rule 13f-1. ⚠️ The widely-repeated "$100 billion" comes from the 2019 Nielsen whistleblower complaint, **not the SEC order** — attribute it separately or drop it.

Also: **Peter's Pence** — a December 2019 *Wall Street Journal* investigation found that of ~$55 million collected annually and presented as a fund for the poor, **only about 10% went to charitable causes**, the rest covering the Vatican's administrative deficit.

### Be fair — the strongest counter-evidence

- **48% of US congregations — over 150,000 — run or support a food distribution program.** Food assistance is the most common congregational program at 52%.
- **Southern Baptist Disaster Relief has 65,000 trained volunteers** and served **14.6 million meals over seven months after Katrina**. A Red Cross president: "We could not fulfill our mission without the Southern Baptists." Essentially every hot meal the Red Cross serves in a US disaster is cooked by Southern Baptist volunteers.
- **Religious Americans give and volunteer more to *secular* causes** — +10 points on nonreligious giving, +21 points on nonreligious volunteering, controlling for income, education, ideology and demographics (Brooks, Social Capital Community Benchmark Survey).
- The over-reporting critique **does not land cleanly**. Church *attendance* is over-reported by roughly half (Hadaway/Marler/Chaves, *ASR* 1993), but Bekkers & Wiepking (2011) found **Protestant respondents were *less* likely than the non-religious to inflate reported donations**, and Rooney/Steinberg/Schervish (2001) found short survey instruments **depress** giving estimates by up to 10× at the median. The Giving USA religion estimate is anchored to institutional revenue reports anyway, not household recall. **Drop this line of attack; the 1986 baseline is the real one.**

### One design idea worth stealing

The 6% "denominational dues" line in NSCEP is a **fixed top-slice for shared infrastructure, decided once rather than per-grant**. That is a genuinely good pattern for Earthbux's newsroom funding — a standing percentage set in the bylaws beats a per-mission negotiation.

⚠️ But do not romanticize congregational democracy. Congregational polity (member vote on the budget) is a *minority* arrangement — Catholic, Orthodox, Episcopal, Methodist and LDS structures have no member vote on spending at all. **A tithe is not evidence of democratic allocation**, and no published study reports what share of US congregations actually vote their budget.

## Part 3 — Section 3 draft: Direct operating charities

### The honest proportion, first

Do not let the cases below imply the sector is corrupt — the arithmetic does not support it. Across **~1.9 million registered nonprofits**, the FTC's entire charity docket since 2009 is a few hundred defendants. The four cancer charities of 2015 raised $187 million — **0.03% of a single year's US giving**. The ACFE finds nonprofit fraud losses run *below* the all-sector median (**$76,000** median loss per detected case vs. $104,000 overall). Nonprofits employ one in ten private-sector workers.

⚠️ **Do not print "nonprofits lose 5% of revenue to fraud."** That is a survey of fraud examiners' *opinions*, not a measurement, and it is not nonprofit-specific. Multiplying it by total giving produces a fake number that circulates widely.

⚠️ And fraud is not rising — **enforcement is**. ([Fortune, May 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/05/24/nonprofit-fraud-doj-enforcement-false-claims-record/))

**The real story is not fraud. It is legal leakage.** Four mechanisms, none of which requires anyone to break a law, and none of which the program-expense ratio can detect:

### Mechanism 1 — professional solicitors

The New York AG's *Pennies for Charity* is the only large, continuous, regulator-audited dataset on this in the country. **2024 data, released December 2025:**

- **~$1.6 billion raised**; professional fundraisers kept **25% — over $395 million**
- **In half of all 588 campaigns (293), the charity received less than 50 cents on the dollar**
- **104 campaigns (18%) lost money outright**, costing charities more than $20 million

The aggregate "75% reaches charity" is misleadingly reassuring — it is dominated by a handful of large efficient campaigns. **The distribution is the story, not the mean.** (Note the 2023→2024 reversal, 83% → 75%; don't report a trend off two points.)

This is legal because *Riley v. National Federation of the Blind* (1988) bars states from capping fundraising costs or compelling percentage disclosure. Enforcement must always be framed as **deception**, never as excessive cost — which is why every FTC case reads the way it does.

Historical anchor: the Tampa Bay Times / CIR **"America's Worst Charities"** (2013) examined 10 years of filings for 5,800 charities and found the 50 worst paid roughly **$1 billion to for-profit solicitors**. ⚠️ The paired "$1.3B raised / under 4% to direct cash aid" figures are only confirmable via secondary sources (original is paywalled) — pull the original before printing those two.

### Mechanism 2 — gift-in-kind overvaluation

**Why it works mechanically:** GIK counts as both revenue and program expense. Inflating the assigned value pushes the program ratio toward 100% while cash overhead stays constant in absolute terms. A charity spending 65% of *cash* on programs can report 95% by loading in overvalued pills.

The deworming-pill scandal is the clearest illustration. Mebendazole/albendazole tablets cost about **2 cents**:

| Charity | Valued at | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Islamic Relief USA (2010) | **$16.25/pill** | most of a reported $182M in gifts |
| Crista Ministries (2011) | $10.64/pill | ~$63M inflated |
| Operation Blessing (2010) | $6.85/pill | ~$300M added |
| Feed the Children (2011) | cut $9.07 → 35¢ | **$668M reduction** in reported GIK |
| *Direct Relief* | **3.2¢** | *(the honest comparison)* |

**Enforcement did follow.** California AG, September 2019: cease-and-desist orders and **$1,490,175** in penalties against **Food for the Poor, Catholic Medical Mission Board, and MAP International**, finding all three implied up to 99% of cash donations went to programs when up to 40% of cash went to administration and fundraising. Food for the Poor's "more than 95% of all donations go directly to programs" was found **"unfair and deceptive"** because it blended cash and noncash — CharityWatch put its actual cash-basis program spending at **65%**, a 30-point gap. FFP separately paid **$300,000** to Michigan in 2018 over the same language, denying the solicitations were deceptive. **World Help** restated $227 million of donated medical goods down to ~$100 million in 2012, after goods actually worth $3.3 million had been booked at $100 million.

**The fix was half-made.** California AB 1181, which would have required valuation at the *end-recipient market*, passed the legislature and was **vetoed by Gov. Newsom in October 2019**. FASB ASU 2020-07 now requires GIK to appear as a separate line with disclosure of valuation technique — real progress on transparency, **but it does not change the valuation**.

### Mechanism 3 — joint cost allocation

Under ASC 958-720-45, if a fundraising solicitation also carries a program message, a charity may split the cost between fundraising and program, subject to three qualitative tests (purpose, audience, content). The standard requires the method be "rational and systematic" but **prescribes no methodology** — so two charities with identical mailings can report wildly different splits.

**The one hard example with numbers**, from the California AG's own published guidance: **National Veterans Foundation reported $7.75 million in program expenses using joint cost allocation in 2009, versus $929,234 without it — a 734% overstatement.** The AG required an amended 990. The AG also names the incentive explicitly: charities game toward **BBB's 65% program-expense threshold**.

### Mechanism 4 — the ratio itself is systematically understated

**Kim, Charbonneau & Sowa, *Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly* (2024)** used the Item Sum Double-List Technique — an indirect-questioning method built to defeat social-desirability bias, because managers will not admit to this directly. Estimate: **nonprofit managers artificially lower reported overhead ratios by roughly 10 percentage points** (range 7–16).

That is a peer-reviewed finding that the single metric donors are told to check is understated by about ten points sector-wide — a decade after the sector's own watchdogs (GuideStar, Charity Navigator, BBB WGA) jointly repudiated it in the 2013 **Overhead Myth** letter: *"The people and communities served by charities don't need low overhead, they need high performance."*

**(C) Legal but scandalous — and the two famous stories that need heavy correction**

**Wounded Warrior Project.** ⚠️ **This is the most over-told story in the file. If you repeat the 2016 framing without the 2016–17 corrections you will be repeating errors that were formally rebutted by both a forensic review and a Republican senator's inquiry.**

*What held up* (Grassley memo, May 2017): the claimed **80.6% program ratio was misleading — real figures 67.5% (FY2014) and 64.5% (FY2015)** once donated media and joint-cost allocations were removed. About **$80.7M (33.3%)** of FY2014 "program expenses" was donated advertising. **$55.1 million** transferred to a long-term trust was counted as program expense but never distributed to veterans.

*What did not hold up:* the "$3 million Broadmoor conference" that drove the coverage was **under $1 million**. Premium-class travel was **under 1%** of paid air travel per the independent review. **94% of $26 million in conference spending was program-related.** Executive compensation was found **reasonable**. The CBS claim that WWP told donors "warriors call us, we don't call warriors" was itself inaccurate — WWP made 150,065 outreach calls. **No fraud was ever alleged, charged, or found by any regulator.** Revenue fell 43% anyway.

**Note what this case actually is:** the same joint-cost and donated-media mechanism from Mechanisms 2 and 3, operating at a large, legitimate, well-known charity. That is why it belongs in the legal-but-scandalous bucket, and it is a *better* story that way.

**American Red Cross Haiti.** "Half a billion dollars and six homes" is literally accurate and was never retracted — but it sets one narrow output (newly built permanent houses) against total funds raised across food aid, shelter, water, health, and hospital construction. ProPublica's own article notes ~4,000 home repairs and $44 million in food aid.

**The stronger, cleaner facts are Grassley's (June 2016):** of $487.6 million raised, **25.4% ($123.9M)** went to internal expenses; **$363.7M passed to partner organizations that then deducted their own administrative expenses** — overhead taken at least twice and never disclosed in total; and the Red Cross **"does not track costs on a project by project basis"** and could not produce per-project costs. Plus the fully documented Campeche/LAMIKA failure: **$24 million budgeted, 700 homes promised by January 2013, zero built.**

**Do not use:** Cancer Treatment Centers of America (a **for-profit**, acquired by City of Hope in 2021 — if it appears on a "worst charities" list, that list is wrong). The Trump Foundation ($2M judgment, 2019) is real but is a **grantmaking shell with no programs** — a self-dealing case, not an operating-charity case.

⚠️ **Southern Poverty Law Center** — an 11-count federal indictment was returned **April 21, 2026** alleging more than $3 million in donor funds routed to individuals associated with the KKK, Aryan Nations, and the American Nazi Party via fictitious entities, 2014–2023. SPLC says the payments compensated informants and calls the case political targeting; DOJ denies political motivation. **No trial, no conviction. Report the counts, the denial, and the context — and nothing more.** Given the political salience, this needs the most careful handling of anything here.

### The regime that doesn't operate

IRC **§4958 intermediate sanctions** (1996) allows a 25% excise tax on excess benefits, 200% if uncorrected. But the **rebuttable presumption of reasonableness** — hire a compensation consultant, benchmark a peer group, minute the vote — makes almost any pay package defensible. And the enforcement numbers are the story: in FY2024 the IRS received **over 1.8 million tax-exempt returns and conducted 666 Form 990-series examinations.** ⚠️ I could not find published IRS data on §4958 excise taxes actually assessed in any recent year — that is a **FOIA target, and the absence is itself a finding.**

**Nearly every meaningful enforcement action in this entire section came from a state attorney general or the FTC, not the IRS.**

## Part 4 — The good, and what Earthbux should copy

You named Good360 and Direct Relief. **Direct Relief survives rigorous scrutiny. Good360 does not survive it equally well** — and the difference is exactly the mechanism in Part 3, Mechanism 2.

### Direct Relief — verified good

FY2025: **$2,411.4M** total support, of which **$2,272.0M (94.2%) is contributed goods**; expenses $2,309.3M at **99.3% program**. CharityWatch **A+, 90% program, $5 to raise $100**. Charity Navigator 4/4, 100%. **Zero government funding.**

**The valuation answer — this is why they pass.** Direct Relief values FDA-approved pharmaceuticals at **Wholesale Acquisition Cost from the Micromedex RED BOOK**, matched **per National Drug Code, refreshed monthly**. Four things make that defensible:

1. **WAC, not AWP** — they state explicitly that AWP runs ~25% above WAC, and reject it. AWP is the classic inflation lever.
2. **Per-NDC matching** — generics get valued as generics, closing the single most common GIK trick.
3. **The price file is external.** The donor does not supply the number.
4. The audited statements and the marketing page state the **same** policy.

And the strongest evidence of all: **Direct Relief was a source for CNN's 2012–2014 investigation into GIK overvaluation by other charities**, handing over a case in which medications they valued at ~$3M were reported by a partner at "more than $100 million" — a 33× markup on identical goods. That is an organization volunteering evidence that damages its own sector's headline numbers.

**Still worth asking:** strip the ~$2,272M of contributed goods from both sides and the residual cash operation is roughly $37M, of which $16.0M is admin and fundraising. Their CEO's FY2025 compensation of **$1,042,426** is 0.04% of total revenue but **0.9% of cash revenue** — the cleanest illustration in this whole file of why GIK-inflated denominators destroy compensation intuitions. Always run that ratio against the cash denominator.

### Good360 — copy the model, not the books

FY2024: revenue **$3,264,744,236**, **99.7% program**, in-kind **98.9% of revenue**. Cash-only, it is a **~$34 million logistics operation**.

**The valuation problem**, from Note 12 of the 2023 audited financials: Good360 "will verify that... **the fair value provided by the donor** is reasonable," and if it looks unreasonable, will revalue "by researching like kind product using **various commercial websites**."

| | Direct Relief | Good360 |
|---|---|---|
| Price source | External commercial price file | **The donor** |
| Charity's role | Applies the file | Reviews the donor's number for reasonableness |
| Granularity | Per NDC, monthly | Per donor description |
| Fallback | n/a | "various commercial websites" — retail-ish, unspecified |
| Stated conservatism | Rejects AWP as ~25% too high | **No stated haircut** |

This matters *more* for goods than drugs: corporate donors of inventory get an enhanced deduction under **IRC §170(e)(3)** — up to twice basis, capped at fair market value. **The donor's tax benefit rises with the stated value, and Good360 accepts the donor's number subject only to a reasonableness screen.**

**A stress test you can run yourself:** the 2024 annual report reports **$3.3B of goods distributed** and **188 million pounds diverted from landfills** — roughly **$17.55 per pound** of surplus consumer goods. Scopes may not be identical and some categories genuinely price high. But for a mix including furniture, apparel and household goods, that number deserves a question. **Ask Good360 for the pounds-to-dollars reconciliation. If they won't provide it, that is the finding.** Note also that CharityWatch — the strictest watchdog — publishes **no public grade** for Good360; for an organization this size, the absence is informative.

**What is genuinely excellent about Good360**, and worth copying outright: the **"second disaster"** problem is real — humanitarian logistics researcher José Holguín-Veras finds **50–70% of goods arriving during emergencies don't fit the recovery**. Refrigerators shipped to Haitians living in tents without electricity. Ten trailers rotting in a Puerto Rico parking lot after Maria. Good360 routes **requested** goods to vetted nonprofits rather than accepting unsolicited donations, and **phases** aid over time — after the 2025 LA wildfires they sent essentials immediately and furniture about seven months later as families relocated. **Phasing is a sophisticated operational idea and directly transferable to a weekly mission cycle.**

### GiveDirectly — the disclosure model

- **>$87 of every $100** to recipients as cash on the marketing page — but **their own blog** reports flagship African efficiency **fell from 82% (end-2022) to ~72%**, with segmented targets: flagship ~85%, bespoke ~75%, **emergency relief ~60%**.
- They **tightened their own metric against themselves twice**: moved the numerator from dollars *committed* to dollars *delivered*, and stopped counting currency-translation adjustments as operational cost because Malawi's devaluation would have *flattered* the number.
- **The evidence base is strong.** Egger, Haushofer, Miguel, Niehaus & Walker, *Econometrica* (Nov 2022): 10,500+ households across **653 randomized villages**, a fiscal shock of **over 15% of local GDP**, a **local transfer multiplier of 2.5**, and **minimal price inflation**. That is the answer to "won't cash just cause inflation," from a top-five journal.
- **The Kenya UBI experiment** found the **lump sum** generated the most new businesses and the largest income gains (~50% over control), while short-term monthly UBI lagged on enterprise but improved diet diversity. **The same total dollars produced different outcomes depending on the payment schedule.** A weekly mission cycle is a schedule choice with real consequences, not an implementation detail.

**The fraud, and why the handling is the model.** Just under **$1.2 million** was diverted from D.R.C. operations in 2022 — 0.8% of the $144M delivered globally that year — by staff conspiring with mobile-money agents to register payment SIMs in recipients' names and keep them. **~1,900 families** lost their first five months of payments. Detected by newly hired internal audit staff in January 2023, about five months in.

What they did: paused D.R.C. operations, referred staff to authorities, engaged Grant Thornton and Humentum as external reviewers, **repaid affected recipients from other funds**, and **announced it publicly on June 5, 2023, proactively**. Contemporaneous coverage reported ~$900,000; the final figure was just under $1.2M — **they revised upward in public**. Then they institutionalized it: a **standing annual incident report** on fraud, abuse and safety, first published June 2025, which states losses run at or under 1% against $150M+ delivered *and* adds the caveat that losses **"could increase as our detection improves."** That caveat is the mark of an honest metric.

### Which evaluator to trust

1. **CharityWatch** — the only one that **recomputes from 990s rather than accepting reported figures**, **excludes non-cash goods** ("follows the cash"), and **reclassifies joint costs to fundraising**. It neutralizes both levers from Part 3. Weaknesses: paywalled, narrower coverage, and it says nothing about whether the program works.
2. **GiveWell** — best-in-class on *whether the program works*, covering a handful of global health/poverty orgs. Top charities (Sept 2025): Malaria Consortium (~$4,000/life saved), Helen Keller Intl (~$3,500), Against Malaria Foundation (~$5,500), New Incentives (~$4,500). **GiveWell states the caveats itself**: estimates are "often highly uncertain," marginal ≠ average, they are historical not predictive, and moral weights are subjective. "$4,000 per life saved" is a modeling output with wide error bars, not a price tag.
3. **BBB Wise Giving Alliance** — best written *standards*, pass/fail so no discrimination among good orgs. The 65%/35% thresholds are floors, not excellence.
4. **Charity Navigator** — broadest coverage, four beacons (Impact & Measurement, Accountability & Finance, Leadership & Planning, Culture & Compensation). ⚠️ **But the GIK hole is real**: it has a "Non-Cash" classification for orgs where 50%+ of contributions are goods — and then applies **the same 85%/50% program-expense thresholds anyway**. It identifies GIK-heavy organizations and does not adjust for GIK. **Never use it alone for a goods-heavy recipient.**
5. **Candid/GuideStar seals** — measure *willingness to fill in forms*, not integrity. A cheap screen, never a rating.

**Use at least two, always.**

### ⚠️ The hard part: does democratic allocation produce worse outcomes?

**Probably yes on effectiveness, probably no on legitimacy and reach.** Earthbux is choosing legitimacy over expected cost-effectiveness. That is defensible — but say it out loud in the founding documents rather than discovering it in year three when a journalist runs the numbers.

**The evidence against:**

- **Small donors actively recoil from effectiveness evidence.** Karlan & Wood (NBER w20047), a field experiment with **34,673 solicitations to 21,643 unique donors**: large prior donors ($100+) gave **more** when shown scientific effectiveness evidence (+2.2pp, +$12.98); **small prior donors (<$100) gave *less* (−1.4pp)**. Pooled, effectiveness information had no effect. **Earthbux's electorate is by construction small donors.**
- **The bias may be evolved.** Burum, Nowak & Hoffman, *Nature Human Behaviour* (2020): people are sensitive to matching multipliers when saving for themselves but not when donating; **observers reward the fact of donating and show no responsiveness to match rates, amounts, or fractions given.** A public, social, visible giving platform sits directly on top of this bias and amplifies it.
- **Crowd allocation reproduces the inequity of the underlying social graph.** Kenworthy et al., *PLOS ONE* (2020), 637 GoFundMe medical campaigns: **only 9.2% met their goal; 77.71% of recipients were white; Black recipients received $22 less per donation** — and the disparities **persisted after controlling for campaign features and social network size**. This is not "the crowd is stupid." It is that a crowd allocates along contours that are already unequal.
- **Porto Alegre is the cautionary tale on durability.** Participatory budgeting there raised sewer and water connections **from 75% to 98%** and health/education's budget share **from 13% to ~40%** by 1997. Participation went from under 1,000 people a year in 1990 to ~40,000 in 1999 — **and then decayed**. When the Workers' Party lost power the successor government deprioritized it; federal money arrived in the 2000s with **no participation requirement**, which actively discouraged the participatory route; PB was suspended in 2017 and has since been eliminated. **Participatory allocation is not self-sustaining.**
- **NYC's version, on the numbers:** 76,434 total votes across 24 of 51 districts in 2024, $1M per district, **no mechanism to ensure a representative sample**, vote tallies not consistently published, no vote-integrity safeguards. One analyst's verdict: "As an outreach program, perhaps. As an actual democratic process, no."

**The counterweight:** Brazilian PB genuinely **shifted spending toward the poor and reduced infant mortality** (Touchton & Wampler 2014; Gonçalves 2014, covering 1990–2004). But note the mechanism — it worked because *the previously excluded* were the ones voting. That is a redistribution effect, not a wisdom-of-crowds effect, and it will not transfer automatically to a platform whose voters are comparatively advantaged.

⚠️ **The single most relevant paper in existence for Earthbux's core design question** is Wojcik, Ford, Hanson, Boyd & Ashley (2020), "Participatory Grantmaking: A Test of Rubric Scoring Versus Popular Voting Selection in a Blinded Grantmaking Process," *The Foundation Review* 12(1). It directly compares popular voting against rubric scoring in a blinded process. **My researcher hit a 403 on the full text. Buy it.**

### What Earthbux should actually copy — prioritized

**Before launch:**

**1. Publish a physical-unit metric alongside every dollar figure.** Direct Relief's **WHO Defined Daily Doses** is the best single idea in this research. Dollars are a *valuation*; doses, meals, nets and pounds are *facts*. Report both on every mission, and where they disagree, the newsroom's job is to say so. This one practice makes the entire GIK inflation problem visible instead of hidden.

**2. Write the disclosure policy before the first failure.** Copy GiveDirectly literally: a standing **annual incident report**; a **public case page** for any material incident; **proactive** publication; **upward revisions published** when numbers grow; and the caveat that detected losses may rise as detection improves. Add GiveWell's **[Our Mistakes](https://www.givewell.org/about/our-mistakes)** structure (two tiers, each entry with timeframe, description, remediation) and Engineers Without Borders Canada's annual **Failure Report** cadence. A newsroom that only covers its own successes is a marketing department.

**3. Make CharityWatch's adjustments the house editorial standard.** For every recipient publish three numbers: the reported program ratio, a **cash-only ratio** with GIK stripped from both sides, and a ratio with **joint costs reclassified to fundraising**. Every time. That is the concrete thing separating Earthbux News from a press-release aggregator — and the checklist below makes it a repeatable 30-minute job.

**4. Protect the vote structurally, and split the ballot.** Porto Alegre died when priorities shifted and unconditioned money arrived from elsewhere — put the voting mechanism in the bylaws behind a supermajority. Then defuse the popularity-bias problem without abandoning democracy:
- **Vetting is a gate, not a vote.** Nobody votes an auto-revoked charity onto a ballot.
- **The community elects the cause; a rubric ranks recipients within it.** This is the FRIDA Fund structure and it is the direct answer to Wojcik et al.'s question.
- **Reserve a fixed share for an effectiveness-ranked slate** (GiveWell top charities or equivalent) exempt from the popular vote. Publish the split. Then publish, annually, how the voted allocations compare to the reserved slate on cost-per-outcome. **That comparison is the newsroom's best recurring story, and it makes Earthbux the first platform generating real evidence on the open question above.**

**First year:**

**5. Publish every grant in an open machine-readable standard.** Adopt or mirror the [360Giving Data Standard](https://grantnav.threesixtygiving.org/) (1,494,534 grants from 352 funders, CC BY 4.0) and imitate Direct Relief's daily-updated Aid Tracker and Aid Map as the presentation layer.

**6. Copy Good360's operating model, not its books.** Match *requested* needs to available supply; never accept unsolicited goods; **phase** aid over time.

**7. Steal the Awesome Foundation's cadence discipline.** $1,000 monthly grants, **69 chapters in 10 countries, $7,927,000 across 7,927 projects** over fifteen years — and it scaled by **replicating small autonomous chapters rather than growing one large electorate**. If your weekly cycle produces voter fatigue (and Porto Alegre says it will), the proven remedy is *more, smaller pools*, not more frequent asks on the same pool.

**8. Adopt BBB WGA standards 15–20 as Earthbux News's editorial code** — solicitation accuracy, annual report contents, website disclosure, donor privacy with annual opt-out, and **standard 19's cause-marketing disclosure** the moment a corporate partner appears. Also standard 10: don't accumulate unrestricted reserves beyond 3× annual expenses. **A weekly-payout platform sitting on a growing balance is a story waiting to happen.**

**Two things to resist:**

**9. Do not claim "100%."** You could run charity: water's model honestly — segregated audited operating fund, separate overhead donors, even reimbursed payment-processing fees. But CharityWatch's critique is correct that the claim is **true of a fund and false of an organization** — and **Kids Wish Network told donors "100% of your contribution benefits the children" while spending 18% on programs**, with fundraisers keeping $12.8M of $14.4M raised. **charity: water and Kids Wish Network are not the same thing, but they use the same sentence, and a donor cannot tell them apart.** Say instead: *"100% of the weekly pool is disbursed to the elected cause. Earthbux's own costs are $X, funded by Y, and here they are."* You lose a marketing line and gain the only thing a newsroom-attached charity actually has to sell.

**10. Do not pretend the democratic trade-off doesn't exist.** See above. State it in the founding documents.

## Appendix — The per-recipient vetting checklist

**Tier 1 — automated, under 60 seconds, disqualifying. Run on every candidate before it reaches a ballot.**

1. EIN resolves in **IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search** and appears in **Pub 78 data** (contributions actually deductible). *Fail = off the ballot.*
2. EIN **not** on the **IRS Automatic Revocation List** (monthly file — three consecutive years of non-filing triggers automatic loss of status). **A revoked org can still have a live website and a working donate button.** *Fail = off the ballot.*
3. No name or officer match on the **OFAC SDN list**. *Fail = off the ballot, and stop.*
4. **ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API** (free, no key) returns at least one filing in the last 24 months. *Fail = manual review.*
5. Pull revenue, expenses, program expenses, net assets, officer compensation. Compute program %; flag below 65% (the BBB floor).

**Tier 2 — human, 20–30 minutes, per finalist.**

6. Open the most recent **Form 990 PDF** and confirm the API numbers match. They sometimes don't.
7. **Part VIII line 1g** — what fraction of revenue is noncash? **If >30%, escalate to Tier 3 and never publish a program ratio without a cash-only version beside it.**
8. **Schedule G Part I** — professional fundraisers: gross raised vs. **amount retained by the fundraiser**. This is where Kids Wish Network's $12.8M of $14.4M shows up.
9. **Schedule L** — related-party transactions (Part I excess benefit, Part II loans, Part III grants to interested persons, Part IV business transactions). **Name the counterparty and amount in the published writeup, always, even when innocuous.** GiveDirectly's ~$560,000 in 2023 fees to TTS, a payment platform formed by its co-founders, is a live example of a clean disclosure of an awkward fact.
10. **Part IX line 26** — joint costs allocated to program? Recompute with the whole joint cost moved to fundraising and **publish both numbers**.
11. **Part VI** — independent board majority; conflict-of-interest policy *monitored and enforced*; whistleblower policy; CEO compensation process.
12. **Part VII / Schedule J** — top compensation against **cash** program spend, never total.
13. **Part XII lines 3a–b** — Single Audit required? If yes, get and read the actual report; the 990 only tells you it exists.
14. Cross-check ratings on **at least two** of CharityWatch, Charity Navigator, BBB give.org. **Never Charity Navigator alone for a GIK-heavy org.**

**Tier 3 — GIK escalation, any recipient over 30% noncash revenue.**

15. **Schedule M column (d) — "Method of determining noncash contribution amounts."** *This is the single most important box on the entire form.* "Donor's estimate" and "third-party published price file" are not the same answer. Direct Relief and Good360 would fill this box differently. **Publish that sentence verbatim.**
16. **Schedule M Part II line 32b** — third party used to solicit, process or sell noncash contributions? A yes plus a big number is a pass-through structure.
17. **Part V line 7 / Form 8282** — if donated property was disposed of within three years, the charity must report what it actually got. **Mismatch between the donor's claimed Form 8283 value and the 8282 sale price is the classic overvaluation detection route.**
18. Ask the organization in writing: *what price source, at what granularity, refreshed how often, and what discount for used or near-expiry goods?* **Publish the answer or publish the refusal.**
19. Run a **physical-unit sanity check** — dollars per pound, per dose, per unit shipped — and publish it.
20. Compute and publish a **cash-only program ratio** beside the headline.
